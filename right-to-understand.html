Citizens' Right to Understand

Who gets to decide how new technologies affect a community? Decisions about AI systems and the infrastructure behind them are already being made in budgets, contracts, zoning votes, and agency agendas. This framework is a practical way to follow one of those decisions from first notice to real participation.

The principle
People affected by technological decisions should be able to understand those decisions in a meaningful way.

The gap we're trying to close

Technology moves faster than most communities can follow it. The distance between the two is the civic capacity gap. Making documents public doesn't close it on its own. A 387-page technical study released ten days before a hearing is technically available and practically out of reach. What closes the gap is a community's ability to find decisions early, understand them, see who they affect, and act while the outcome can still change.

The Participation Framework

Six steps. You won't always move through them in order, and the last step feeds the first: what you learn changes what you watch for next time.

Worked example

Data centers in Prince George's County, Maryland

In July 2026 the County Council adopted a two-year moratorium on data centers, the longest in the state. Three sites are already named: Landover Mall/Brightseat, Konterra in Laurel, and a study area in Brandywine. A pending bill, LDR-155, is the likely vehicle for eventually ending the pause.

Case facts come from the Maryland Data Center Governance Tracker, current as of September 1, 2026. Labels show how solid each claim is: VERIFIED from a primary or cited source, ASSESSMENT our analysis, UNKNOWN not established in public sources. Check the tracker for updates before you act.

1

Detect

Notice a decision, policy, contract, or proposal that may affect your community, ideally before it's final.

Where decisions show up

Budget line items, council and school board agendas, zoning applications, procurement contracts, executive orders, and state agency dockets. Most decisions surface in a document months before they reach the news.

In the case
  • VERIFIEDMoratorium adopted July 7, 2026, backed by County Executive Order No. 1-2026.
  • VERIFIEDLDR-155 is pending with no hearing date scheduled yet.
  • ASSESSMENTThe next decision point isn't the moratorium, which is already law. It's LDR-155. That bill is the thing to watch.
Ask: What's the next point where this decision can still change, and how will we know when it's coming?
2

Translate

Turn technical, legal, and bureaucratic language into plain language that anyone affected can follow.

In the case
  • "Moratorium" means a temporary pause. New data center approvals stop for a set time, usually while rules get written.
  • "Off-ramp" means the rule that would end the pause. Whatever LDR-155 says becomes the rulebook for what comes next.
  • "By-right" means a project that meets the written rules gets approved automatically, with no case-by-case hearing. ASSESSMENT Whether LDR-155 creates by-right paths for some data centers is one of the key questions in the case.
Ask: Could a neighbor, a student, or a grandparent explain in two sentences what this decision does? If not, translation is the first job.
3

Map Rights & Rules

Figure out which laws, policies, and procedures govern the decision, who holds authority, and what rights residents have in the process.

In the case
  • County Council holds zoning authority. It passed the moratorium and will vote on LDR-155.
  • County Executive issued a supporting executive order.
  • Water and sewer: UNKNOWN The position of WSSC, the regional water utility, on watershed impact isn't established in public sources.
  • Tax incentives: VERIFIED Exemptions passed in 2020 still shape the economics. At least one council member has said she wants them repealed, but no formal repeal bill has been confirmed.
  • Your rights: Marylanders can request public records under the Maryland Public Information Act, and zoning bills go through public hearings.
Ask: Who actually decides? What rules are they bound by? Where is the public guaranteed a say, and where isn't it?
4

Map Impacts

Work out who benefits, who carries the risk, and who's missing from the conversation.

In the case
  • UNKNOWN Water, grid, wastewater, and road capacity have not been established in public sources for any of the three sites.
  • VERIFIED About 46% of Maryland, including Prince George's, was in extreme drought in late June 2026.
  • UNKNOWN Jobs and tax revenue figures, plus site-level size and power use, aren't in the public record the tracker could find.

The gaps are a finding in their own right. When residents can't see capacity or benefit numbers, they're being asked to weigh a tradeoff with one side of the scale hidden.

Ask: Who benefits? Who bears the cost? Are they the same people? Who hasn't been asked?
5

Act

Use the formal channels for participation, oversight, and challenge.

Pathways

Public comment and testimony, public records requests, advisory boards, contacting elected officials, organizing through civic associations or PTSAs, and elections.

In the case
  • Watch for the LDR-155 hearing notice and sign up to testify or submit written comments.
  • File a public records request for the missing site-level details and developer terms.
  • Ask WSSC directly for its assessment of watershed impact.
  • Ask your council member where they stand on by-right approvals and on the 2020 tax exemptions.
Ask: Which action fits where the decision is right now? Testimony matters most before a vote. Records requests take time, so start early.
6

Learn

Find out whether participation changed the outcome, and carry that forward.

In the case
  • After LDR-155 is voted on, compare the final text to what residents asked for. What changed and what didn't?
  • Compare with neighbors. VERIFIED Howard County passed a 17-month moratorium and is staffing a task force to write rules before any proposal arrives. Harford passed a permanent ban whose legal durability is contested.
  • Record what you learned about timing, access, and who showed up, so the next decision is easier to follow.
Ask: Did anyone's participation change anything? If not, where did the process close before the public got in?

Try it on a decision near you

Pick something happening in your school district, county, or state: a budget item, a new technology contract, a zoning proposal. Work through the six steps alone or with a group. Your notes stay on this page and aren't saved anywhere. Print it before you leave.

Want to practice first? The Patuxent County Civic Simulator puts these steps into a game. Its "What Didn't We Know?" challenge is about the Right to Understand.
